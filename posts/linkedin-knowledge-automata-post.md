In my last post, I said the first case study was in the oven. It's out.

Knowledge Automata is an open-source project I built in my personal time, over two weekends, some late nights and a fair amount of coffee. AI agents turn the raw notes a group produces into validated records, each traceable to its source.

It runs on @Cohere: Command for the language model, Embed and Rerank for search. The stack was smooth to work with, and the best part was the open docs, which my agents could read directly.

It was the first real test of my 16 principles. As in any project with agents, I was going to start by writing instruction files, such as AGENTS.md. Instead, I pointed the agents at the principles repo, we negotiated what we needed, and they wrote the files themselves, linked to the principles. What grew from there was so organic! A way of working that kept improving itself, with every lesson written down for the next session. The side effect that surprised me most: my agents kept me closer to my own principles.

At the start of any project, everyone agrees on good practices. Two months later, busy with the work, we forget. Human attention is limited, and discipline is expensive. Sometimes it doesn't even feel feasible. Here I saw the opposite. My agents reread the rules every time, in seconds. When I drifted, they pushed back: that's out of scope, according to the backlog. I'd already said it. I'd just forgotten. They kept me accountable to my own decisions.

We externalized the project's memory, and its process too.

Reading is remembering.

Same four pillars as last time, now with real examples:
I. Think before you generate: the backlog became a contract.
II. Generate responsibly: review went every direction. AI reviewed AI, I reviewed the AI, and the AI reviewed me.
III. Prove it works: assumptions were labeled "not proven yet" until we tested them.
IV. Build for the future: core docs, written once, for humans and AIs alike.

None of that is new. What has changed is what it costs to follow it.

What failed, failed where systems meet: a line I missed in an API's docs, and a file a tool said it saved but didn't. Agents move faster than people can absorb, so my next experiment is to slow down at the boundaries.

In my first post I asked: how much engineering discipline can we afford? Turns out, quite a lot! And it was still fast. Alone, as a robotics engineer and not a traditional software developer, I'd probably still be setting up the repo 😅.

Want to try it yourself? Point your agents at the principles repo, and let the cooking begin.
